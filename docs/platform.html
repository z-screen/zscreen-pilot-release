Z-Screen Pilot Release/ Platform
ABOUT

The platform behind these preprints.

Z-Screen is a way to make many new molecules and immediately ask living cells what those molecules did. Instead of moving compounds through plates, robots, follow-up assays, and separate sequencing workflows, the chip keeps the molecule, the image, and the RNA readout tied together in one tiny well. The five preprints show what that can already do at pilot scale.

What Z-Screen does

A chip, not a robotics line. A Z-Screen chip contains tens of thousands of microwells. Each well holds a small group of cells and one bead carrying one compound. Once the chip is loaded, the experiment happens in place.

Two readouts from the same well. First the chip images the cells. Then it reads the RNA from that same well. The image tells you what changed. The RNA helps explain which biological programs moved. Paper 01 tests whether those two views work better together than alone.

Chemistry with memory. Z-Screen compounds are built from known chemical building blocks. That means a hit is not just one isolated molecule; it can be traced back to the pieces that created the response. Papers 02 and 03 use that structure to predict new combinations and test whether the model is learning rather than copying.

Scale by repeating the unit. The chip is the throughput unit. More campaigns can be run by running more chips, without turning every program into a custom robotics buildout. That is why the pilot matters: the early signal came from a small slice of the platform's intended capacity.

Platform comparison

What sits in the same conversation, and how Z-Screen is different.

Other platforms are strong on one axis: very large imaging screens, deep single-cell sequencing atlases, or compound-reference catalogs. Z-Screen is different because it keeps three things together: the molecule's recipe, the image of the cell response, and the RNA state from the same well.

IMAGING ONLY

Cell Painting / Recursion / Insitro / JUMP

These platforms can see morphology at enormous scale. The usual follow-up question is what the morphology means biologically. Z-Screen keeps the image, then adds an RNA readout from the same well.

SINGLE-CELL PERTURB

Perturb-seq / Tahoe-100M / scPerturb

These atlases are deep references for how cells respond to perturbations. Z-Screen's difference is chemistry resolution: each measured response is attached to the building blocks that made the compound.

CONNECTIVITY MAP / LINCS L1000

Compound ↔ gene-program search

LINCS made compound-to-gene-program search useful for the field. Z-Screen adds a design layer: because the compounds come from a known combinatorial recipe, the response can be connected back to chemical choices.

DEL / BELKA

DNA-encoded library ML

DEL datasets ask whether a molecule binds a target. Z-Screen asks what the molecule does to a living cell. Paper 03 uses the DEL literature's hard lesson about generalization: separate real learning from close-analog lookup.

What Zafrens does with it

Zafrens uses Z-Screen as a discovery engine across modalities, including small molecules, peptides, macrocycles, PROTACs, nucleic-acid delivery, cell therapies, ADC optimization, and patient-cell models. The common thread is the same: make or present many candidates, read the cell response, and let the next experiment become more informed than the last.

The preprints are not a detached academic exercise. They describe the measurement and modeling layer underneath active Zafrens programs, and they show why Z-Screen can also be useful as a data-generation engine for partners.

Why this release

A pilot is the right time to show the operating thesis.

A platform story should not live only in a deck. These five preprints put the early evidence in writing, with one public dataset and reproducible analysis code. The pilot is small by design, and each paper answers one foundational question:

  • 01Can we measure what a compound does and read why, from the same well, at scale?
  • 02Does combinatorial chemistry yield a learnable design map, not a static catalog?
  • 03Does the model generalize to new chemistry, or is it memorizing the training set?
  • 04Does the response transfer across cell types we never directly assayed?
  • 05Can an unknown hit point to a testable mechanism hypothesis?

Across the release, the answer is yes with the right boundaries. Some results are ready to scale; others define the next decisive experiment. That is the point of publishing the pilot: the signal is visible, the caveats are explicit, and the next step is no longer vague.
