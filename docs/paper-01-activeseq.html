PAPER 01 - ActiveSeq

One well can show what changed and why it changed.

Drug screens often give you either a picture of what happened to cells or a molecular readout of which biology moved. ActiveSeq asks for both from the same tiny well. In this pilot, combining the image and RNA views made compound classification better across the control panel, which is the first step toward hits that arrive with biological context instead of mystery.

TL;DR

ActiveSeq reads an image and an RNA profile from the same microwell. On 35 control compounds, image plus RNA classified held-out wells better than RNA alone or image alone. The important point is practical: a Z-Screen hit can carry both visible phenotype and molecular context from the start.

Why it matters

In early discovery, the fastest screens often leave teams asking what a hit actually means. Imaging can scale, but morphology alone can be hard to translate into biology. RNA is more explanatory, but it is usually too expensive to run deeply across large compound libraries.

ActiveSeq puts the two readouts together. A Z-Screen hit can come with an image of the cell response and an RNA fingerprint from the same small colony of cells. That does not remove every follow-up experiment, but it gives teams a much better starting point.

What we did

We analyzed two HEK293 pilot runs with 20,222 matched wells. A 35-compound control panel tested whether the readout could recover known compound identity. Dummy-bead wells acted as a built-in stress test for assay chemistry.

For each well, we compared the image signal, the RNA signal, and the combined signal. The questions were straightforward: does the assay catch bad wells, are the readouts reproducible, do image and RNA organize compounds in related ways, and does combining them help on held-out wells?

We also placed the result next to scGeneScope, a public treatment-matched imaging plus RNA benchmark. That comparison is for scale and context, not a direct win/loss claim, because the experimental designs are different.

What we found

Five results that make same-well readout useful.

FINDING 01

Bad wells are visible.

The dummy-bead wells, which should not behave like real compound wells, showed the expected collapse in RNA yield. That matters operationally because failed chemistry does not have to quietly enter the biology model.

FINDING 02

The same compounds look like themselves again.

When the wells were split into pseudo-replicates, both RNA and image features recovered compound identity far above chance. The signal also transferred across the two pilot runs, which is the basic reliability bar for a screening readout.

85.7% / 100%Cross-run top-1 / top-3 - image
FINDING 03

The two readouts agree, but they are not copies.

Image and RNA organized the compound panel in related ways. That is exactly what you want: enough agreement to trust the biology, enough difference that combining them can add information.

ρ = 0.718Image ↔ RNA agreement
FINDING 04

Putting image and RNA together helps.

On held-out wells, the combined readout beat image alone and RNA alone. The gain was broad across the control panel, not driven by a few easy compounds.

35 / 35Compounds improved by fusion
FINDING 05

The public benchmark puts the effect in context.

scGeneScope is larger and built differently, so this is not a direct contest. After adjusting for task difficulty, the pilot's multimodal effect sits in the same broad range as that public reference, which supports continued scale-up.

What this enables

A better first look at every hit.

For a discovery team, ActiveSeq changes the first question after a screen. Instead of asking only "which wells looked interesting?", the team can ask "what did the cells look like, and which molecular programs moved?" The pilot does not prove every future same-well use case, but it shows the readout is reproducible, additive, and interpretable enough to justify scaling the matched image-plus-RNA workflow.
